# Damian on basecamp.com — sample B

## Page /
- Your headline is nineteen words long. Nobody reads nineteen word headlines. They scan the first four words and check out. You are burying the one thing that might make them stay under a pile of adjectives.
- The yellow highlighted phrases inside your testimonials look like you went through with a marker after the fact. Real people do not talk in highlighter-ready soundbites. This makes the whole social proof section feel manufactured rather than earned.
- Your primary button asks for three minutes. That is a lifetime on a first visit. People who do not know you yet will not commit three minutes based on one headline. You are losing them before they click.
- You are showing eight testimonials at once. Nobody reads eight testimonials. They read maybe two, then their eyes glaze over and they scroll past the entire section. You are wasting your best proof here.

## Page /pricing
- Your two pricing columns have different numbers of features listed. Eleven on the left, eight on the right. When someone tries to scan across and compare, the right column ends early and feels like it is missing things even when it is not.
- The text 'Rock-solid reliability' at the bottom of your features list renders at 4.28 to 1 contrast ratio. That is below the 4.5 threshold that normal text needs. Anyone with moderate vision strain or a slightly glarey screen will squint at it or skip it entirely.
- That Tobi Lutke quote sits to the left of your pricing cards but it does not connect to them visually or structurally. A visitor deciding between the two packages has to ignore it to focus on the actual choice. Social proof works best right next to the action it supports.

## Page /customers
- The yellow background on phrases inside every testimonial looks like keyword extraction, not human emphasis. It signals that these quotes were processed by a script rather than curated, which undercuts the authenticity you need from social proof.
- Every testimonial has the same dimensions, same font size, and same visual weight. A visitor sees a uniform block of text with no entry point and no way to judge which peer story matters to their situation.
- You have names and companies but no photos of the people speaking. On a page built entirely on trust and social proof, removing the human face removes the emotional hook that makes someone stop scrolling and read.
- The Try Basecamp free button sits in the bottom right corner unconnected to any particular testimonial or section. It floats visually separate from the content it relates to, making it easy to miss or dismiss as an overlay ad.

## Page /support/testimonials
- The yellow highlights on phrases like 'Very impressed' and 'Clearly not an AI bot' read as if an algorithm picked them or a template defaulted them in. Real people emphasizing their own words do not highlight this way, and calling out 'not an AI bot' in a highlighted block actually makes me wonder if it is one.
- Every review is signed with a first name and last initial. Jenny C., Casey C., Harry M. I have no idea what these people do, what size company they run, or whether their situation resembles mine at all. I cannot calibrate whether their praise means anything for my decision.
- Every testimonial card is the same size, same styling, same visual weight. My eye lands nowhere because everywhere is equal. I see a wall of praise and I skim none of it. You have fifteen chances to make someone feel something and right now you are spending them all as if they are worth the same amount.
- The 'Try Basecamp free' button sits in the bottom right like it arrived late and found whatever seat was left. This entire page exists to build enough confidence that someone clicks that button, yet it is positioned where someone who already decided would find it, not where someone still deciding would see it.

## Page /apps
- You have Sign in and Try Basecamp free sitting side by side at the bottom with nearly identical visual weight. A first time visitor does not know which one is for them and will hesitate or pick wrong.
- Your Windows and Mac buttons are small generic blue rectangles while the App Store and Google Play badges above them are large branded images. Desktop feels like you stopped caring halfway down the page.
- You write Or, see our guide on progressive web apps. That opening word frames PWA as the consolation prize rather than a legitimate way people might want to use your product.

## The board — what to build, cut, or charge for

### Stop competing with Slack on chat  (High impact, 1w)
**Problem:** You built Campfires and Pings for group messaging, but every team already lives in Slack or Teams. This feature will never be why someone picks Basecamp, and maintaining real time chat drains your evenings while adding no retention value.
**Do:** Remove Campfires as a standalone feature. Keep only the ability to comment on tasks and ping someone about a specific item. Point users to Slack integration instead. Spend that time making the Automatic Check-ins feature impossible to live without.

### Build a weekly team health report  (High impact, 3d)
**Problem:** Your customers keep saying 'transparency' and 'accountability' and 'nothing gets missed.' But there is no automated way for a manager to see who is falling behind without logging in and digging around. That friction means they stop checking.
**Do:** Create a Friday afternoon email summary showing: tasks completed this week, overdue items, who has not checked in, and upcoming deadlines. Make it forwardable to leadership. This gives managers a reason to keep their team on Basecamp and creates a weekly touchpoint without you sending marketing emails.

### Add a simple client portal view  (Medium impact, 1w)
**Problem:** Testimonials say clients are happier and dealing with projects is easier. But clients still need full accounts and see internal chatter. You are missing the agency/freelancer use case where clients just want to see progress, approve things, and leave comments without seeing your team argue about deadlines.
**Do:** Build a read mostly view for external guests that shows only: active tasks assigned to them, files shared, deadlines, and a comment box. No access to internal chat, no ability to create projects. Charge $5 per guest per month on top of any plan. This turns Basecamp into a client management tool, not just an internal one.

### Sell the flat price harder  (High impact, 4h)
**Problem:** Your $299 per month for unlimited users is your only real pricing advantage against per user tools like Asana and Monday. But on your pricing page it sits next to the per user option like they are equal. A team of 20 pays $300 elsewhere and $299 here. That should be the headline.
**Do:** Rewrite the pricing page to lead with a calculator: enter your team size, show what Asana/Monday costs, show what Basecamp costs. Put the $299 plan first and make the per user plan look like the backup option for tiny teams. Add a 'switching from per user pricing saves you $X per year' callout near the trial button.

### Cut the generic testimonial wall  (Low impact, 2h)
**Problem:** Your customer pages are walls of identical quote cards. Nobody reads past the third one. A church committee and a genomics company have different needs, but you present them the same way. A new visitor cannot find someone like them.
**Do:** Group testimonials by team type: agencies, internal teams, nonprofits, small business. Show three max per group. Put the most specific result at the top of each group ('we cut status meetings from 3 hours to 15 minutes'). Remove the star ratings because every single one is five stars, which tells me nothing about what is actually different here.
